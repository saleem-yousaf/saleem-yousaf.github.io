Motivators

Lines worth carrying into a hard day.

Short motivators from sport, tech, leadership and life. Not a book to get through, just the line, where it came from, and why it still holds up when things get difficult.

MOTIVATOR 01
Fight like cornered tigers.
Imran Khan  ·  Pakistan, Cricket World Cup 1992

The cornered tigers, 1992

Imran Khan, Pakistan's World Cup winning captain, later Prime Minister, now imprisoned.

Pakistan began the 1992 World Cup badly. They lost matches, one game was saved only by rain, and by the group stage they were close to going home. Most captains would have managed the exit. Imran Khan did the opposite. He told his players to stop fearing the loss and to play without it, to wear green and fight like cornered tigers, an animal that is most dangerous when it has nowhere left to run.

The side turned. They won when they had to, reached the final at the Melbourne Cricket Ground, and beat England to lift the trophy. The phrase outlived the tournament because it named something true about that team: they were at their most capable when they had the least to lose.

The corner is not the end of the fight. For a side with nothing left to protect, it is the start of one.

Why it holds up, as a life skill. Being cornered, by a setback, a loss, a hard season, strips away the comfortable options and leaves only the real one: respond. Fighting like a cornered tiger is not about violence. It is about refusing to fold. You get up, you use the position you are actually in, and you move. The corner sharpens you precisely because there is no soft way out of it.

MOTIVATOR 02
Service to others is the rent you pay for your room here on earth.
Muhammad Ali  ·  the cause beyond the ring

The cause beyond the ring

Muhammad Ali, born Cassius Clay, heavyweight champion, and for a time one of the most recognisable people alive.

In 1967 Ali refused to be drafted into the Vietnam war. He objected on principle, and he said plainly that he had no quarrel with people on the far side of the world while his own were treated as less than equal at home. The cost came at once. He was stripped of his heavyweight title and banned from boxing for more than three years, through the physical prime of his career. The Supreme Court cleared him in 1971, but by then the years were gone, and he never asked for them back.

He had already changed his name and refused to be told who to be. Standing up as a Black man in America in the 1960s, at the height of his fame, when silence would have been far safer and far more profitable, is the part worth remembering. He fought a cause, not only opponents.

What came after the ring carried as much weight as anything in it. Ali gave an estimated 22 million meals to feed the hungry, campaigned for debt relief for the poorest countries, many of them in Africa, and served as a United Nations Messenger of Peace, hand delivering food and medicine into places most people avoid. A good deal of it he did quietly, including tuition grants the people who received them were never meant to trace back to him.

Wars of nations are fought to change maps. But wars of poverty are fought to map change.

Why it holds up, as a life skill. A platform is not a prize, it is rent. Whatever you are good at, whatever gets you noticed, is worth most when you spend it on someone else. And standing up for a cause can cost you your title, your best years and the easy path, and still be the right call. Ali paid that rent in full, and the name outlived the fights because of it.

MOTIVATOR 03
We don't want your money, we want your voice.
Bob Geldof  ·  Live Aid, Live 8, and the reunion no one else could get

The man who brought the impossible together

Bob Geldof, Boomtown Rats singer, who turned a famine most governments were ignoring into the biggest show on earth. Twice.

In 1984 Geldof saw the Ethiopian famine on the news and refused to leave it there. He wrote Do They Know It's Christmas with Midge Ure, then built Live Aid: a concert staged at Wembley and in Philadelphia at the same time, on 13 July 1985, put together in about ten weeks and watched by an estimated 1.5 billion people. It raised more than a hundred million dollars for famine relief, and it forced Africa onto a political agenda that had been looking away. Nobody had run a global concert on two continents at once. He did it anyway.

Twenty years later he did something harder. Live 8, on 2 July 2005, was a run of concerts staged around the world on the same day, and this time it was not about the money. It was aimed squarely at the G8 leaders meeting at Gleneagles, to push them to cancel the debts of the poorest countries and double aid for Africa. As he put it, this time he did not want your money, he wanted your voice.

And he pulled off the thing no one else could. For Live 8 he got Pink Floyd's classic lineup, Roger Waters, David Gilmour, Nick Mason and Richard Wright, onto one stage for the first time in more than twenty four years, across the most bitter feud in rock. Gilmour said no. Geldof got on a train to his farm, brought in Nick Mason to reach Waters, then quietly passed Waters the number so the two men would speak directly. They played four songs in Hyde Park, dedicated to Syd Barrett. It was the last time those four ever played together. Richard Wright died three years later. Only Bob could have made that happen, and it never happened again.

The impossible is usually just a stack of noes that no one has worked through yet. He worked through them.

Why it holds up, as a life skill. Most people stop at the first no. Geldof treated no as the start of the conversation, not the end of it. Getting people who cannot stand each other into the same room, for something bigger than any of them, is worth more than talent. Whether you have a platform, a contacts book, or only a refusal to be told it cannot be done, the lesson is the same: the immovable moves when someone decides it has to.

And leaders worth following are rare.

A line like that only works because someone stood in front and meant it. Good leaders, the kind people choose to follow into a hard day, are in short supply. Seek them out, learn from them, and where you can, become one. We need more of them, not fewer.

CLOSING
He stood between a friend and the arrows, and spent his last breath on loyalty.
Boromir, to Aragorn  ·  The Lord of the Rings

His dying words gave his captain three names at once: my brother, my captain, my king. A fitting close, the follower, at the end, naming the leader worth following.
